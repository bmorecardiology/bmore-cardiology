## 1. Taking your blood pressure at home

Placement: `articles/hypertension.html`. Demonstration: upper-arm cuff, seated position, sample log.

“A useful blood pressure reading starts before you press the button. Use an upper-arm monitor with the right cuff size. Avoid exercise, nicotine and caffeine for thirty minutes beforehand, empty your bladder, and sit quietly for five minutes.

Keep your back supported, feet flat, and arm supported at heart level. Put the cuff on bare skin and stay quiet while it measures. Take two readings, one minute apart, and write down both numbers from both readings. Keeping only the lowest can give a misleading picture.

Ask your clinician how often to measure and when to share the record. The tracker on this website helps organize readings, but it does not send them to your care team or monitor you.

An average should never hide an urgent problem. If your top number is above 180 or your bottom number is above 120 with concerning symptoms, call 911. The full guide explains what to do with a very high reading when you feel well.”

End card: `bmorecardiology.com/articles/hypertension.html`.

## 2. What a calcium score does—and does not—tell you

Placement: `tests/calcium-score.html` and related condition guide. Visual: simple labels “plaque,” “risk,” and “prevention plan”; no invented scan.

“A coronary calcium scan looks for calcified plaque in the heart arteries. It can help answer a prevention question, such as whether cholesterol-lowering treatment would be useful when that decision is uncertain.

The result is a score, not the percentage of an artery that is blocked. A higher score generally means more plaque. A score of zero means no calcified plaque was found, but it does not exclude all plaque or mean you should ignore new symptoms.

The next step is to put the number alongside your cholesterol, blood pressure, family history and other conditions. Ask what the result changes about your treatment and follow-up.

Do not start aspirin from the score alone. We also need to consider bleeding risk and your overall situation. Do not stop a prescribed treatment without discussing it.

Bring your questions and use the one-page guide to prepare. Severe new chest symptoms need emergency assessment, not a screening scan.”

End card: `bmorecardiology.com/tests/calcium-score.html`.

## 3. Reading an echo report

Placement: `tests/echocardiogram.html`. Visual: report terms as text; no patient report.

“An echocardiogram uses ultrasound to look at the heart muscle, chambers and valves. A standard echo through the chest does not use ionizing radiation.

One number you may see is the ejection fraction, or EF. It is the percentage of blood the left ventricle pushes out with each beat. It is not the percentage of your heart that is working. A preserved EF also does not rule out every form of heart failure.

Other report words can sound alarming. Regurgitation means leakage through a valve, and stenosis means narrowing. A mild finding does not automatically mean you need a procedure. We interpret it with your symptoms, the rest of the study, and sometimes previous scans.

Three useful questions are: What are the important findings? Does anything need follow-up? And does the result explain my symptoms?

A resting echo does not answer every question about coronary arteries. If symptoms continue, discuss them even after a reassuring report.”

End card: `bmorecardiology.com/tests/echocardiogram.html`.

## 4. Getting the most from your rhythm monitor

Placement: `tests/rhythm-monitor.html`. Demonstration: blank symptom diary and a sample device, if available.

“Palpitations or dizziness may come and go, so an office ECG can miss an episode. A patch, Holter or event monitor records your heart rhythm during daily life.

Follow the instructions for your exact device. Wear time, showering, activity and return instructions differ. Keep a record of symptoms, their time, and what you were doing. If your device has an event button, use it as instructed.

The report may describe early beats, short rhythm episodes, or a burden—the amount of a particular rhythm in the recording. A label alone does not determine treatment. We look at the rhythm strips, symptoms and the condition of your heart.

Ask when to return the device and how you will get results. Some monitors are analyzed after return, so do not assume yours is watched continuously. Pressing the button is not a request for emergency help.

For severe chest symptoms, fainting, stroke symptoms or severe trouble breathing, call 911.”

End card: `bmorecardiology.com/tests/rhythm-monitor.html`.

## 5. A useful conversation about statins

Placement: `articles/statins.html` and `blog/statin-controversy.html`. Visual: “benefit,” “symptoms,” “plan.”

“If a statin is recommended, you deserve to understand why it is likely to help you. The expected benefit depends on your cholesterol, previous heart or vascular disease, and other risks.

You also deserve a plan for symptoms. Muscle aches can have several causes, and a normal muscle blood test does not automatically exclude statin-related symptoms. Tell your clinician when symptoms started and which medicines or supplements you take.

The SAMSON trial compared statin tablets, placebo tablets and months with no tablets in sixty people who had stopped statins because of symptoms. Similar symptoms during statin and placebo months showed that taking a tablet and expectations can contribute. It did not mean symptoms were imaginary or that statins never cause adverse effects.

The goal is a treatment you can tolerate that provides the protection you need. Options may include a different statin, dose, supervised schedule or another medicine. Make those decisions with your clinician instead of creating your own dosing plan.”

End card: `bmorecardiology.com/articles/statins.html`.

## 6. Start moving from where you are

Placement: `articles/getting-started-exercise.html` and `blog/exercise-is-the-best-medicine.html`. Demonstration: comfortable walking; no vigorous exercise prescription.

“You do not have to start with a race or a gym membership. If you feel well and have no condition that limits activity, a short, comfortable walk can be a useful beginning.

Choose a time and activity you can repeat. Five or ten minutes counts. Build consistency and time before making the activity much harder. During moderate activity, you can generally still talk, even though you are breathing harder.

If you have known heart disease, a recent procedure, new symptoms, or a goal of vigorous training, ask your clinician for an individual plan. A watch target is not a universal prescription, especially when medicines change your heart rate.

After some cardiac diagnoses and procedures, cardiac rehabilitation can offer supervised activity, education and support. Ask whether you qualify.

Stop for chest discomfort, marked dizziness or unusual breathlessness. Severe or persistent chest symptoms, fainting or severe breathing difficulty need emergency care. The best starting plan is one you understand and can safely repeat.”

End card: `bmorecardiology.com/articles/getting-started-exercise.html`.
