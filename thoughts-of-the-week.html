Hypertension: Myths and Realities

How to measure your blood pressure correctly and why those numbers actually matter for your health

I spend a significant portion of my clinic day correcting how patients measure their blood pressure. And I don't say that to criticize — most people have never been taught the right way. But here's the problem: if you measure incorrectly, you get meaningless numbers. And you can't manage what you can't measure.

Today I'm going to teach you exactly how to measure your blood pressure so that you, your doctor, and I are looking at reliable data. This matters more than you think.

Myth #1: You Should Check Your BP Every Day

Reality: You should measure it no more than once per week.

Here's why: Blood pressure is not a static number. It changes constantly — every second, every minute. It's influenced by:

  • Time of day (lower in morning, higher in evening)
  • What you just ate
  • How much coffee you've had
  • Whether you just exercised
  • Your stress level
  • Your anxiety about the measurement itself (yes, really)
  • Even room temperature

Checking every day creates noise — lots of different numbers that don't tell you anything useful. What matters is the trend over time, not day-to-day variation. One high reading means nothing. A pattern of elevated readings over weeks and months means something.

Check once per week. That's it. Pick a day (say, Saturday morning) and that's your day.

Myth #2: One Blood Pressure Reading Tells You Your Status

Reality: You need 3 readings taken over 5 minutes, and you write down the LOWEST systolic number.

Here's the protocol I recommend to all my patients:

The Right Way to Measure BP

  1. Sit quietly for 5 minutes — No talking, no phone, no distractions. Let your body settle.
  2. Take 3 readings, 1-2 minutes apart — Use the same arm, same position each time
  3. Write down ALL 3 numbers — Don't just record the average; record all three
  4. Use the LOWEST systolic number — This is key. The first reading is often elevated from activity. As you relax, it comes down. That lower number is more representative of your true resting BP.
  5. Record this in a log — Use our app or a simple notebook. Date it, time it.

Why the lowest number? Because BP naturally drifts down as you sit quietly. The lowest reading during your quiet sitting session represents your actual resting blood pressure. The first reading is often inflated just from the act of measuring and the anticipation.

Myth #3: You Need to Check Your BP at Home

Reality: You should, but ONLY if your equipment is validated and you're using it correctly.

Home BP measurement is valuable — sometimes more valuable than office readings, because you're relaxed and not in a doctor's office (which itself raises BP for many people). But:

  • Buy a validated device (most pharmacies have a list; ask which ones they recommend)
  • Use the same device every time
  • Measure on your upper arm, not your wrist (wrist monitors are notoriously inaccurate)
  • Keep your arm at heart level
  • Don't measure over clothing
  • Use the same arm each time (BP can differ between arms by 5-10 points)

If you don't want to buy a monitor, that's okay. Many CVS/Walgreens pharmacies have free machines. You can also ask your doctor's office if they'll take readings for you periodically.

The Chart: How to Track Your BP Over Time

Here's where the rubber meets the road. I want you to track your BP over at least 3 months (12 weeks = 12 weekly measurements). This gives us a real picture of your average and your trend.

Sample BP Log

Week Date Reading 1 Reading 2 Reading 3 Lowest Systolic Notes
1 4/19 142/88 138/85 136/83 136 After exercise
2 4/26 138/86 134/83 132/81 132 Normal rest
3 5/3 135/84 131/82 129/80 129 Trending down

After 12 weeks, you'll have 12 data points. Average those lowest systolic numbers. That average is your true baseline BP. Now we can have a real conversation about whether you need medication, whether your medication is working, or whether lifestyle changes are paying off.

Our Virtual Cardiology app has a built-in BP tracking feature. You can log your readings directly and see your trend over time. No spreadsheet needed. No guessing. Just Courses → Rate → Vital Signs and record your BP along with your other measurements. It's right there.

Myth #4: My BP is High Because I'm Nervous at the Doctor's Office

Reality: "White coat hypertension" is real, but that doesn't mean high BP doesn't matter.

Yes, many people have elevated BP in the office and normal BP at home. That's white coat hypertension. BUT — and this is important — these patients still have an increased risk of developing sustained hypertension and cardiovascular disease.

So white coat hypertension isn't a "false alarm" that you can ignore. It's a yellow flag. It means:

  • Your autonomic nervous system is reactive to stress
  • You need to work on stress management
  • You need regular home BP monitoring to see the real picture
  • You may benefit from lifestyle modifications (exercise, meditation, better sleep)

So yes, measure at home. But do it right — using the protocol I described above.

Myth #5: Blood Pressure is Binary (You Have It or You Don't)

Reality: Hypertension exists on a spectrum, and the goal is individualized.

For most people, the target is below 130/80 (the 2017 ACC/AHA definition of "normal"). For some people with diabetes or kidney disease, the target might be lower. For very elderly people, it might be slightly higher (to prevent falls and low BP).

The point is: this isn't one-size-fits-all. Your doctor should know your readings, understand your risk profile, and set a goal that makes sense for YOU.

So Here's What I Want You to Do

  1. Get a validated home BP monitor (or use your pharmacy's free machine)
  2. Pick one day per week to measure (Saturday morning, for example)
  3. Measure 3 times, 1-2 minutes apart, sitting quietly beforehand
  4. Write down the lowest systolic number
  5. Log it in our app or a notebook — with the date and any notes (stressed? just exercised? etc.)
  6. After 12 weeks, bring your log to your doctor appointment
  7. Discuss the trend with your doctor. This is the real conversation.

This isn't just busywork. This data — your actual, accurately-measured BP numbers over 3 months — is what allows your doctor to make smart, personalized treatment decisions. It's the difference between guessing and knowing.

Why This Matters for Your Health

High blood pressure is called the "silent killer" because you don't feel it, but it's silently damaging your arteries, your heart, your kidneys, and your brain. The only way to know if you have it is to measure. And the only way to know if treatment is working is to track carefully over time.

You deserve to be the expert on your own numbers. Not just knowing them, but understanding them. Measuring them correctly. Seeing your trend. Discussing them intelligently with your doctor.

That's how we catch problems early. That's how we prevent heart attacks and strokes. That's how we add healthy years to your life.

Exercise Is the Best Medicine — Here's Why
The single most underrated tool for extending healthy life, preventing disease, and living younger than your age.
Read More →

I see something every day in my practice that frustrates me: people taking handfuls of medications to manage conditions that could have been prevented or reversed with exercise. Meanwhile, they tell me they "don't have time" to work out.

Let me be direct: If you have time for disease, you have time for exercise. Because exercise isn't optional for health — it's foundational. More than any medication, any supplement, any diet alone, exercise is the single most powerful tool we have for extending healthy life.

The Evidence Is Overwhelming

The data are unambiguous. Multiple large studies have shown that:

  • Regular exercise adds 3-7 years to your lifespan — and more importantly, adds healthy years, not just extra time in a hospital bed
  • Exercise reduces cardiovascular death risk by 30-40% — comparable to or better than most medications we prescribe
  • Sedentary behavior is a risk factor comparable to smoking — sitting is literally killing you
  • Exercise prevents or reverses type 2 diabetes in 90% of early-stage cases, without medication
  • Regular physical activity reduces cancer risk by 20-30% across multiple cancer types
  • Exercise improves cognitive function and reduces dementia risk by 35% in older adults

These aren't small effects. These are life-changing, disease-preventing, year-extending effects. And they're free.

How Much Exercise Do You Actually Need?

The WHO and ACC/AHA guidelines are clear:

  • 150 minutes per week of moderate-intensity aerobic activity (think: brisk walking, steady cycling, jogging) OR
  • 75 minutes per week of vigorous-intensity activity (running, high-intensity intervals, competitive sports) OR
  • A combination of both
  • PLUS 2 days per week of muscle-strengthening activities (weights, resistance bands, bodyweight exercises)

That's it. 150 minutes. That's 30 minutes, 5 days a week. Or an hour-long workout 3 days a week. For most people, this is absolutely achievable.

And here's the thing: more is better. People who exercise 300+ minutes per week see even greater benefits. But even 75-100 minutes per week — the bare minimum some people can do — provides substantial protection.

Why Exercise Works Better Than You Think

Exercise doesn't just "burn calories" or "keep weight down" (though it does both). Here's what actually happens when you exercise regularly:

🫀 Cardiovascular Benefits

  • Blood vessels become more flexible and produce more nitric oxide (the "Viagra of the arteries")
  • Blood pressure drops, sometimes by 10-15 mmHg with regular aerobic exercise
  • HDL cholesterol increases, LDL cholesterol decreases
  • Triglycerides plummet
  • Inflammation markers (CRP, IL-6) drop significantly
  • Heart rate variability improves (a marker of autonomic nervous system health)
  • Atherosclerosis progression slows or reverses

🧠 Brain & Cognitive Function

  • Exercise increases BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) — essentially "fertilizer" for your brain cells
  • New neurons are literally born in the hippocampus (memory center) with regular aerobic exercise
  • Blood flow to the brain increases
  • Memory, processing speed, and executive function all improve
  • Depression and anxiety risk drop by 25-30%
  • Dementia and Alzheimer's risk plummets

💪 Metabolic & Endocrine Effects

  • Insulin sensitivity improves dramatically
  • Fasting glucose normalizes
  • Pancreatic beta cells (insulin producers) recover function
  • Metabolic rate increases
  • Mitochondrial density increases — your cells produce energy more efficiently
  • Hormone levels normalize (testosterone, growth hormone, cortisol)

🦴 Muscle & Bone

  • Muscle mass is preserved or increased (critical for aging well)
  • Bone density increases, preventing osteoporosis
  • Falls risk decreases due to improved balance and strength
  • Functional independence is maintained longer into old age

🛡️ Immune Function

  • NK (natural killer) cell count and function increase
  • Infection risk decreases
  • Cancer risk drops across multiple cancer types
  • Inflammatory markers decrease
Think of exercise as a medication that: Lowers blood pressure, improves cholesterol, prevents diabetes, reduces cancer risk, improves mood, sharpens memory, strengthens bones, preserves muscle, and extends lifespan. And it has almost zero side effects (when done properly). No drug exists that does all of this.

The Type of Exercise Matters

Not all exercise is equal. You need variety:

  • Aerobic/Cardio: Walking, running, cycling, swimming, rowing. Gets your heart rate up, builds cardiovascular fitness. 150 min/week moderate or 75 min/week vigorous.
  • Strength/Resistance: Weights, resistance bands, bodyweight. Builds muscle, increases metabolism, protects bones and joints. 2x per week, all major muscle groups.
  • Flexibility/Mobility: Yoga, stretching, tai chi. Maintains range of motion, reduces injury risk, improves balance. Daily or most days.
  • HIIT (High-Intensity Interval Training): Alternating hard effort with recovery. Superefficient — you can get big benefits in 20-30 minutes. 2x per week is plenty.

The best exercise is the one you'll actually do. If you hate running, don't run. Walk, swim, cycle, dance — whatever gets you moving consistently.

But I'm Sedentary / Out of Shape / Too Old...

I hear every excuse. "I'm too old to start." "I'm too out of shape." "I have bad knees." "I don't have time." Here's the truth:

  • It's never too late to start. Studies of 70+ year-olds who begin exercising show significant improvements in cardiovascular fitness, strength, and longevity. You don't need to have been athletic your whole life.
  • Start where you are. Even 10-minute bouts of activity add up. Even slow walking counts. You don't have to run a 5K tomorrow — just start walking today.
  • Work around limitations. Bad knees? Try swimming or cycling. Mobility issues? Tai chi and gentle strength training work wonders. Talk to a physical therapist about modifications.
  • Make it nonnegotiable. You make time for work, for your family, for your phone. Exercise isn't less important — it literally extends the healthy years you have to spend with people you love.

The Real Barrier Isn't Time — It's Priority

Here's my observation: people who say they don't have time for exercise somehow find time to binge-watch Netflix, scroll through social media, or sit in traffic. The issue isn't time — it's priority. And priorities reflect values.

If you value your health, your longevity, your ability to play with your grandchildren, to travel in your 70s, to stay independent and sharp — then exercise has to be nonnegotiable. Not "when I have time." Not "when I feel like it." Every single week, scheduled like a doctor's appointment.

Here's a reframe: You don't "find time" for exercise. You make it. You schedule it. You show up. Because the alternative — declining health, disease, reduced lifespan, lost independence — is far worse than the mild inconvenience of taking a 30-minute walk.

What Does This Mean for Cardiac Longevity?

In the framework of cardiac longevity — living more healthy years of life, optimizing heart function, preventing disease — exercise is foundational. More important than any supplement, any special diet, any medication.

A person who exercises consistently, eats reasonably well, manages stress, and sleeps 7-8 hours has better cardiovascular outcomes than someone taking five medications while remaining sedentary.

Exercise + statin (if indicated) + blood pressure control + good nutrition + sleep + stress management = the formula for extended healthy life.

Your Move

If you're currently sedentary: start tomorrow. 30 minutes of walking. That's it. Tomorrow and the next day and the day after that. In a month, you'll feel better. In three months, your bloodwork will improve. In a year, you'll have added years to your healthy lifespan.

If you're already exercising: great. Push a little harder, add strength training if you're not doing it, stay consistent.

Because the question isn't whether you have time for exercise. The question is whether you have time — and whether you'll actually have it to enjoy — if you don't.

The Statin Controversy: Separating Fact from Fear

Why statins remain one of the most effective and safe medications in cardiology — and why the myths about side effects persist

Statins are among the most prescribed and studied medications in the world. They've saved millions of lives. Yet they're also among the most feared. In my practice, I encounter patients weekly who refuse statin therapy because of concerns about muscle pain, cognitive effects, or "toxins" they've heard about online. Today, I want to set the record straight.

Why Statins Work (and Why They Matter)

Statins lower cholesterol by inhibiting an enzyme in the liver that produces LDL cholesterol. More importantly, they do far more than just lower numbers — they stabilize plaques in arteries, reduce inflammation, improve blood vessel function, and prevent heart attacks and strokes.

The evidence is overwhelming. Major studies like the 4S Trial, WOSCOPS, CARE, and PROVE-IT have demonstrated that statins reduce cardiovascular events by 25-50% depending on the patient population. For someone with established heart disease or high cardiovascular risk, a statin is as close to a "magic bullet" as modern medicine gets.

The Side Effect Reality

Here's what the data actually show:

❌ MYTH: Statins cause serious muscle damage in most patients
✅ FACT: Serious muscle injury (rhabdomyolysis) occurs in 1-2 per 100,000 patients. Most muscle complaints are either nocebo effect or are unrelated to the statin.
❌ MYTH: Statins cause cognitive decline and memory loss
✅ FACT: Large studies (including the PROSPER trial with 5,800+ older adults) found NO increased risk of cognitive decline. In fact, by preventing strokes, statins may PROTECT cognitive function.
❌ MYTH: Statins cause diabetes
✅ FACT: Statins may slightly increase diabetes risk in some patients (~9% relative increase), but this is a small effect AND the cardiovascular benefit vastly outweighs any diabetes risk. A diabetic is far better off on a statin than unprotected from a heart attack.
❌ MYTH: Natural alternatives work just as well
✅ FACT: Red yeast rice, plant sterols, and other supplements show modest cholesterol reduction but NO evidence for preventing heart attacks or strokes. They're adjuncts at best, not replacements.

Understanding the Nocebo Effect

Here's something fascinating: in clinical studies comparing statins to placebo (fake pills), patients reported muscle pain and other side effects in BOTH groups — at similar rates. This reveals something powerful about how our minds work.

What is the nocebo effect? It's the opposite of placebo. Just like a sugar pill can make you feel better because you believe it will (placebo), a harmless pill can make you feel worse because you're worried it will hurt you (nocebo). Your expectations literally change what your body feels.

Think of it like this: If you read online that a medication causes headaches, and then you take that medication, your brain becomes hypervigilant. Every minor ache gets interpreted as a headache. Every slight discomfort is blamed on the drug. You're not making it up — you genuinely feel it — but the feeling is amplified (or even created) by fear and expectation.

This doesn't mean all side effects are "in your head." Some people genuinely have statin side effects. But the nocebo effect explains why so many people report symptoms that disappear when they switch to a different statin or when they don't know they're taking a statin at all.

The Real Side Effects (That Matter)

Statins do have side effects. Here's what's actually documented:

  • Muscle myalgia (muscle pain): 5-10% of patients, usually mild, often reversible by switching statins or reducing dose
  • Liver enzyme elevation: Rare and reversible; monitoring with labs is standard
  • New-onset diabetes: 1 in 300 patients, but cardiovascular benefit >> diabetes risk
  • GI upset: Occasional, usually mild

Compare this to the PREVENTED side effects: heart attacks avoided, strokes prevented, lives saved. The math is clear.

Who Should Be On a Statin?

According to current ACC/AHA guidelines, statins are recommended for:

  • Anyone with a history of heart attack, stroke, or coronary disease
  • Diabetic patients age 40-75
  • Adults age 40-75 with LDL ≥ 70 and 10-year ASCVD risk ≥ 7.5%
  • Anyone with familial hypercholesterolemia

For these patients, the question isn't "Should I take a statin?" It's "What's the lowest effective dose and which statin works best for me?"

If You're Statin-Intolerant

Yes, some people genuinely don't tolerate statins. What then?

  • Try a different statin: Pravastatin and rosuvastatin have different side effect profiles than atorvastatin
  • Lower the dose: Even 10mg pravastatin daily provides meaningful benefit
  • Intermittent dosing: Alternate-day or weekly dosing sometimes improves tolerance
  • Ezetimibe: Different mechanism, can be used alone or with statins
  • PCSK9 inhibitors: For high-risk patients who can't tolerate traditional statins (expensive, but highly effective)
Bottom line: Statins are not perfect, but they are among the safest and most effective medications we have. For anyone at risk of heart disease or stroke, the benefits vastly outweigh the risks. Don't let fear and misinformation rob you of protection you need and deserve.

What This Means for Your Cardiac Longevity

In the context of prevention and longevity, statins are a foundational tool. They don't replace lifestyle changes — but they complement them powerfully. A statin + exercise + good nutrition + stress management + sleep creates a synergistic effect that truly extends healthy life.

If you've been avoiding a statin due to fear, I encourage you to have an honest conversation with your cardiologist about the evidence, your specific risk factors, and what the data actually shows for someone like you.

Because the biggest risk isn't taking a statin. It's not taking one when you need it.
